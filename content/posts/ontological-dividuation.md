[Ontological dividuation](/definitions/ontological-dividuation) can happen many different ways; but there is only one way in which two [entities](/definitions/an-entity) are separate: a distinction in [suppositum](/definitions/suppositum). In other words, only by way of a real distinction between two *wholes* can you have separation which is real *and external*. 

For example, the wheel of a car is really distinct from the car *but* it is distinct in an *internal* manner (as the car's [part](/definitions/a-part)). Whereas, Socrates is really distinct from the car in a manner that is *external*. Now, we may initially try to recognize this real, external distinction by way of a distinction in parts; afterall, Socrates shares no parts with the car. However, there can be two suppositum that share parts (e.g., Siamese twins are two different completions and yet share, e.g., the same legs). On the other hand, we may say it is a separation in [substance](/definitions/substance) that distinguishes Socrates and the car; but two suppositum can likewise share a substance (e.g., angels may share the same concrete substance, etc.) and two entities can have the same substance (e.g., two humans in different bodies). Then, we may resort to [relations](/definitions/a-relation) to explain it: by example, two atoms may be identical *except* for their location in space and yet we would typically still say they are really distinct atoms. However, without derailing too much into the metaphysics of space, it is possible that an atom could be represented in two places at once if the laws (or substance) of space allow it. Likewise, the *a priori* space which our cognition uses to represent sensations of objects (for us to have a conscious experience) could represent one object as two in space (even if this ultimately is an error in our faculties of representation). Moreover, depending on the rules of what is causing the relation it can clearly allow for the same object to be represented as multiple. For example, my body is represented in time as multiple moments and yet it is of the same real body I have: the 'me' from three seconds ago is not really a distinct 'me' from now. As seen, the issue is that a relation merely relates two 'things' together; but that does not inherently require that the two 'things' aren't the same 'thing' in reality. Thusly, relative opposition under laws of relations (such as time or space) cannot itself ontologically dividuate. 
  
Although the distinction in suppositum is the definite factor of dividuation which we may not always know for certain, we can infer ways by which two entities would have to be distinct because it would violate metaphysics. For example, if there is an entity with two forms, then that is really two entities; because a form is the actualizing principle which provides the intelligibility of an entity. So two actualizing principles would necessitate two entities with two substances—not one. Likewise, two entities that share a substance but have two different natures cannot be really two: a substance is the underlying reality instantiated *with* the form and, as such, is not ontologically separable from the form (and we already demonstrated a form implies a distinct entity). We might, then, want to posit two entities that share two different substances, but they would have to share a suppositum to complete both types of substances to be related; and this would imply that there is one completion—not two. 

From this, we can note some important things. Firstly, two of the same type of form can only be instantiated in distinct substances *if* they differ in matter. It is worth noting that this does not mean that, e.g., Siamese twins cannot share in matter as distinct in form: it means that they both imperfectly received by the same matter which is really not completely shared (e.g., they have two different heads, some brain matter differences, or whatever it may be). If the Siamese twins truly had the exact same body in all parts, then it would not be possible for there to be two different forms there. Secondly, we can note that immaterial entities would have to be distinct by way of suppositum or form because they have no matter: two supposita of the same essence (which would get instantiated in the same kind of form) without being recieved in matter leaves no leeway for two concrete instances of substance or form. Thusly, e.g., two angels of the same essence would necessarily share the same concrete substance. 
  
Thirdly and finally, we can say that God cannot be instantiated twice in Substance. God is an [essent](/definitions/an-essent): an absolutely simple existence taken as an entity, which has no matter nor anything like it that would allow for dividuation. Thusly, two essents would be ontologically indistinguishable from each other. The only real distinction possible in God is in suppositum.  
  
## Can an Immaterial Entity Have Parts?
  
It is worth noting that an immaterial entity *can have* parts; however, since they are pure form, the only candidate is their existence and this can only be valid if they get it extrinsically. In other words, an immaterial entity which does not have an essence which entails existence would have two parts: essence and existence. However, since an immaterial entity has nothing which can receive form (viz., matter), there is nothing else other than form and existence. In the case of God, He is the only kind of entity which has identical essence and existence.
