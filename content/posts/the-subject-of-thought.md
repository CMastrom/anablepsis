A thought contains a [subject](/definitions/subject) and [object](/definitions/object). The subject does not need to refer to something new: e.g., I can think of the already existent chair or tree in terms of its pre-existing suppositum. Nevertheless, each thought I have is contemplation within itself a completion of some object; so if I think of the tree twice, the structure of each thought is that they each have a subject (and object): not that the subject in intellect is the same. The subject in each refers to the same object, but they are distinct in the structure of thought.

If the act of thought is not identical to the [confernment of existence](/definitions/existential-confernment) (such as is the case with humans when we think), then the completion which we refer to is just the very suppositum which it corresponds to in reality (e.g., the tree of which I am thinking of as my subject of this thought). However, if the act of thought is identical to conferring existence, then the completion in thought is *itself made real* by merely it being thought as real. So if the object of thought is contemplated as real, then so, too, is the subject. In this simple but absolutely powerful mode of thinking which God has, He confers existence to as many supposita as subjects that He thinks of when contemplating an object as real. Those supposita, likewise, cannot fail to be given existence by Him since He is purely actual.

By thinking of Himself as real, then, He of His Substance with a new act of thought which, in turn, is a new completion of contemplation: a new subject (in thought) referring to already existing suppositum of His Substance (which is later identified as the Father). By thinking of Himself suppositally by thinking of Himself as the subject of His object, He simultaneously apprehends Himself as a pre-existing suppositum (i.e., the Father) and must give existence to another suppositum (i.e., the Son); exactly because the perfect union of the subject of thought and the giving of existence resides in this pure act of self-knowing. His subject as Himself does not merely *correspond* to what is real: it, by thinking of the subject as real *for that object*, He gives existence to a subject of that object: the subject itself cannot be [absorbed](/definitions/existential-absorption) by the pre-existing suppositum (i.e., the Father) because it is just, by definition, the completion of the Divine Substance. 

This also answers two questions in one. Firstly, that God must know Himself not just in Substance but in pre-existing suppositum (which attests to His supreme omniscience) by having Himself perfectly as the subject *and not just object* of His thought; and, secondly, that this doesn't merely cause a correspondence between Himself as subject and suppositum (which would cause no other Persons) because His contemplation of 'self' within the mode of reality must also confer existence (not just abstract contemplation in the sense we do).

How, then, doesn't the incarnation produce another Person? God would be thinking of Himself as the subject for the human substance as the object (thusly causing a hypostatically union); and, by the previous reasoning, that thinking of the subject as real would have to also confer existence to that subject for the object, which would be, then, a new suppositum--not any of the pre-existing Three. The answer lies in that when God already knows of an entity which is real, He continues His contemplation from that active knowledge: He does not have discursive reason--only noetic reason. His understanding of everything is perfectly synthesized, from top to bottom, in one absolute idea. I speak of multiple ideas in Him only analogically.

Thusly, when God incarnated Himself, He already knew of Himself completely (as the three Persons possessing the same Substance); so in thinking 'further' of Himself as subject to think of this human substance as His object, He did not begin a 'new thought' but rather 'continued' with the understanding of it. By analogy, God is keeping you in existence right now by actively thinking of you. When you 'further' thinks of you, such as that He changes your shirt to blue from red, He does not start a new thought about you: He continues with His active thought of you which was already there. This does not cause any new suppositum because there is no new thought. The active and absolute thinking of everything in one idea is God perfection in knowledge; but when we speak of the Trinity, we are speaking of the atemporally unfolding of God *gaining* His self-knowledge 'for the first time'. Thusly, His 'original' self-contemplation is not of something He already knows (albeit it atemporal, so He knows all of this from the beginning).

How, then, does the Holy Spirit really become caused, as a new Person, from God's knowledge of the second aspect of Himself; namely, His perfection in oneness? In "[Divine Self-Knowledge](/posts/divine-self-knowledge)" and "[The Trinity](/posts/the-trinity)", we understood the Holy Spirit to be caused from God knowing Himself *after already knowing Himself* that caused the Son; so wouldn't that imply this same 'continuing' of thought which does not cause another suppositum? The answer lies in the fact that God's self-knowledge of the Son's pure unity (viz., perfection) does not cause the Holy Spirit: the collapsing of this thinking of Himself as perfection to Himself willing Himself as perfect does. The mere understanding of Himself, as we conceptualize it in [virtuality](/definitions/virtuality), does not cause another Person because He is contemplating what He already has a conception of. However, in thinking of Himself as perfect He does will Himself as perfect; which makes Him love Himself (as noted in "[The Trinity](/posts/the-trinity)"), and that is identical to Himself willing the Son's existence '*again*'.
